## 7.1 Why Psychology Is the Most Important Lesson

This is the most important lesson in this entire day-trading course.

Successful trading rests on three factors:

1. **A trading system** that gives you an edge in the market — one with a **positive expectancy** that specifies your entry rules and exit rules.
2. **Position sizing** — ensuring that you risk a small percentage of your capital on each trade, and that your **average win is greater than your average loss**.
3. **Psychological discipline** — at least **60%** (or more) of trading success comes from the ability to follow the system consistently and execute your entry and exit rules without deviation.

Many people fail at the third factor. Emotions such as **fear** and **greed** cause them to change their rules halfway, ignore their rules, or abandon the plan because of opinions and rumors they hear in the market. That is the primary reason most traders fail.

---

## 7.2 The Market Wizards Lesson

In Jack Schwager's book *Market Wizards*, he interviewed some of the best traders in the world — people who consistently generated **50% to 100% returns every single year** — to discover their secrets.

What he found was that **their strategy was not their secret**, because they all used completely different systems:

* One trader used **purely technical analysis** — chart reading, no fundamentals.
* Another used **pure fundamentals** — no charts at all.
* Another used **Bollinger Bands** and price-action patterns.
* Another trader thought Bollinger Bands did not work and used **MACD** instead.

They all used completely different entry and exit rules. So what made them all consistently successful? **The answer is that they all had the right psychology in trading.**

A trading seminar or mentor can teach you an exact system used by a millionaire trader, but **you will still lose money** if you do not adopt the same psychology. Most beginners spend their time searching for the right strategy, the right indicator, or the right technique — when they should be adopting the **right mindset** and learning to think differently from the crowd.

> Winning traders are not defined by their indicators or technical knowledge (although those are important). What makes them different is **the mindset they have towards trading**.

The best strategies and indicators will not help you succeed if you have the wrong mindset. Many beginners fall into what is called the **beginner cycle** — going from book to book, seminar to seminar, looking for the best technique that will help them win all the time. No such strategy or indicator exists that can predict the market and win every single time. **Until you accept that, you will never succeed.**

> "The answers to the question of successful trading are to be found in the trader's own mind. Those who seek answers from the latest invention of trading systems or tips will not find answers, because they have not understood the question."
> — Jesse Livermore

So the secret to trading success **does not lie in an amazing theory or indicator**; the secret lies in the **mind of the trader**.

## 7.3 The Core Traits of Winning-Trader Psychology

### 7.3.1 Absolute Discipline

**Discipline** is the ability to trade consistently according to your own entry and exit rules that have been predetermined.

Examples of pre-committed rules:

* When the **50 moving average crosses the 150 moving average**, you buy — and you do this consistently.
* If your trading system says a **bullish pin bar below the lower Bollinger Band** is a buy signal, you take that trade consistently.
* You **never listen to rumors**.
* You ignore the **opinions of other people**.
* You **never buy or sell based on emotions**.

You must also have the discipline to **stick to your strategy through both the wins and the losses**. Even a strategy with a **60–70% win rate** will inevitably go through losing streaks — sometimes three, four, or even ten losses in a row.

When a losing streak begins, most traders feel doubt: *"Is it working? Maybe my strategy isn't working."* Because humans instinctively avoid pain, they are tempted to:

* Change their strategy.
* Add a new indicator.
* Listen to a different guru who "seems to win all the time."

The moment you start intervening in your system or changing your rules, **your system loses its positive expectancy** — and you will lose money in the long run.

### 7.3.2 Ignore Experts and News Predictions

As a winning trader, **you must ignore all the opinions of other people**. When commentators on CNBC or the news declare "the S&P is going down this year" or "oil is going to crash," ignore all of it. **No one can predict the future.** All you can do is follow your strategy and your system, and the money will come.

**Example:** Imagine it is the beginning of 2016 and your strategy says: "Buy on an uptrend, where an uptrend is defined by the 50 MA crossing above the 150 MA." During the first half of January, you read headlines such as:

> *"A recession worse than '28 is coming!"*
> *"S&P will plunge 75% on China deflation!"*

You start to feel fear and doubt. When the 50/150 MA crossover signal appears, you hesitate: *"Maybe I shouldn't take this signal — there could be a crash."* You skip the trade. By the end of the year, the **S&P is up roughly 10%** — and you have missed the entire move.

The lesson: have the discipline to follow your strategy through wins, losses, news, and rumors. That is a key to success.

## 7.4 The Five Pillars of Winning-Trader Psychology

### 7.4.1 Think Statistically

Think like the owner of a casino — you are **not concerned about the specific bets** (whether each one wins or loses), because you know that **if you have a positive expectancy, you will always win in the long run**.

You must think statistically over a **large sample of trades**, not just one, two, or a few. Even a winning strategy produces both wins and losses, and they sometimes come in streaks of five to ten in a row.

* **On a winning streak:** don't get greedy. Do not increase your bet size, risk, or take low-quality trades. The moment you do, you compromise your system and lose in the long run.
* **On a losing streak:** don't get fearful. Do not refuse to take the next trade even though there is a valid signal. Do not reduce your risk or change your indicator to avoid the losses. The moment you do, **your system no longer has a positive expectancy**.

Every individual trade outcome is **statistically insignificant**. Each trade is random — like tossing a coin. A good trade that you executed perfectly can still end up as a loss. **Focus on following the rules consistently**, not on the outcome of each trade.

As long as your strategy gives you an edge and your average win is greater than your average loss, **you will always be profitable at the end of the day over many trades**. Think statistically.

### 7.4.2 Be Patient

All great traders are **extremely patient**. Only trade when you see a **high-probability opportunity** where every one of your entry rules is satisfied.

New traders are often **trigger-happy** — they look for trades every single day, and if none of their setups show up, they get impatient and say: *"To hell with it, I'll just trade anyway."* They want action, they want fun.

If you want fun and action, **go to Disneyland or Universal Studios**. Trading is **not supposed to be fun and exciting**. Trading is supposed to be **boring and mundane**. If it is exciting, **something is wrong**.

* If your rules say there is nothing to enter, **don't enter**.
* In trading, **not doing something is doing something**.
* It is better to **not trade than to take a low-quality trade**.

### 7.4.3 Focus on What Is Happening, Not Why

Focus on **what is happening** in the markets, **not why something is happening**.

New traders often enter a long setup, get stopped out, and immediately start asking *"Why did it hit my stop loss? Was it because Trump said something? Was it because of an interest-rate decision?"* They want to assign blame or find a reason. Other times, they hit a profit target and rush to find a reason for the win.

**This is a complete waste of time as a trader.** Price movements are **not caused** by public news, although it may seem that way. Price movements are caused by **the market's interpretation of the news**.

* You can never predict the news (you cannot predict whether Trump or Clinton will win, whether Brexit will pass, etc.).
* Even if you could predict the news, you cannot predict **how the market will interpret it**. The same news can send prices up in one context and down in another.
* Analysts will always invent an explanation for the move afterwards — but that is entertainment, not trading.

**Just focus on existing price action** and don't ask why something has happened.

### 7.4.4 Do Not Try to Predict the Future

Never attempt to predict the future. When you place a trade, don't tell yourself *"This trade is going to win — it's definitely going up."* Don't make statements like *"I know the S&P is going down this year."*

You should not predict the future because:

1. It is **impossible** — all price movements are driven by **crowd psychology**.
2. **Predictions cloud your judgment** and make you less willing to take losses because of the ego's need to be right.

Beginners who predict a winner then watch it lose become emotionally attached — they feel disappointed, upset, and angry. Worse still, when they say *"I predict it will go up"* and it goes down, they tell themselves *"the market is wrong, I'm right"* and refuse to take the loss. They move their stop loss, give the trade "more room," and **end in absolute disaster**.

Trading is **not about being right or wrong**; it is about **making money or losing money**. Whenever you place a trade, tell yourself: *"I have no idea whether this will be a winning trade or a losing trade. I don't care."* Emotionally detach from any single outcome, because in the long run your wins will outnumber your losses.

### 7.4.5 Stick to a Consistent Risk Percentage

There is **no trade that is guaranteed to win**. There are no sure-win trades or investments. Therefore, **always risk a small percentage of your capital** — typically **1% to a maximum of 3%** per trade.

Once you decide on your risk-per-trade percentage, you must **stick to it consistently**. You cannot say:

* *"I feel good about this trade — I'll risk 3% or 10%."*
* *"I don't feel good about this one — I'll risk 1%."*

If you vary your risk, your system will not have a positive expectancy.

## 7.5 Confidence — The Most Important Quality

### 7.5.1 The Right Kind of Confidence

One of the most important qualities of winning traders is a **very high level of confidence** — but it is not the kind of confidence most people imagine.

Many people think confidence means: *"I place this trade and I know it is going to go up. I am confident of that."* That is the **wrong kind of confidence**, because:

* If you have confidence in a particular trade and it does not work, you get disappointed and upset.
* That smashes your confidence.
* 40–50% of the time (or more) you can still get a losing trade, even with a valid signal.

### 7.5.2 System-Based Confidence

That kind of confidence does **not** come on a trade-by-trade basis. Winning traders have confidence in their **trading system** — confidence that their system has a **positive expectancy**.

* Any single trade could be a win or a loss.
* But if you trade over a **large sample** — like a casino — you will always win in the end.

That is where confidence comes from. Knowing that you will always win in the long run means a losing trade — or even a series of losing trades — does not bother you at all.

> Winning traders believe the **game is won even before they have begun**.

That belief gives them the psychology to grind through their entire system regardless of short-term outcomes.

## 7.6 Good Trades vs. Bad Trades

To succeed as a trader and make money, **all you have to do is ensure you only take good trades**.

| Type | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Good trade** | A trade where you **follow the rules** of your predefined trading strategy that has a positive expectancy. |
| **Bad trade** | A trade where you **break your own rules** — you entered when the rules said don't, or exited when the rules said don't. |

Important distinction:

* **A good trade can be either a winning trade or a losing trade.**
* **A bad trade can also turn out to be a winning trade** (sometimes breaking the rules and still making money).

Executing **good trades consistently** is what guarantees long-term repeatable profitability. But breaking your rules and making money is called **"luck"** — it feels good in the short term but is **extremely damaging psychologically** because it reinforces bad habits of not following your rules. Over the long term, that guarantees you will **not** be profitable.

**Only take good trades. Avoid all bad trades.**

## 7.7 Managing Emotions While Trading

Trading exposes you to a constant flood of emotions tied to money at stake in your account. Because money is an emotional subject for most people, you will experience:

* **Greed**
* **Fear**
* **Doubt**
* **Frustration**
* **Excitement**
* **Hope**

To become a winning trader, you must learn to **think and act contrary to your natural instincts and emotions** — because many of our default emotional patterns cause us to lose money.

The human mind is wired in such a way that it causes the average person to lose money:

* Humans have a need to **avoid pain and avoid being wrong**.
* Humans have a need to **feel pleasure and be right**.
* This drives most people to **follow the crowd, buy high, sell low**, and lose money.

You should be doing the opposite — **buying low and selling high**.

### 7.7.1 Trade at a Level of Capital You Can Emotionally Handle

One of the best ways to trade unemotionally is to trade at a level of capital and risk **where a few losses do not hurt you emotionally**.

If you find yourself rushing downstairs every morning to check your account balance — *"Did I make money? Did I lose money?"* — you are trading with **too much risk** or **too much capital beyond your emotional threshold**. The moment you get very emotional, you will definitely lose money because you can no longer think objectively.

Trade with a small risk such that **even five or ten losing trades don't affect you emotionally**. That is how you trade logically and profitably.

## 7.8 The Five Common Emotions That Destroy Traders

### 7.8.1 Greed

Greed can cause you to **raise your profit targets too high** or **not take profits when you should**, staying in the trade too long. This turns potential winning trades into losing trades.

> "Bulls make money, bears make money, but **pigs always get slaughtered**."

**Example:** You buy a stock with a profit target of 2R. The price hits your target and you exit, then proceeds to go much higher. You feel regret: *"I shouldn't have taken profits so fast — I missed out."* You get greedy. Next time you raise your profit target to 4R. You buy, the price goes up but reverses before 4R, and you end up losing. **A winning trade became a losing trade because you changed your rules out of greed.**

### 7.8.2 Fear

Fear can cause you to **take quick profits** for fear of losing them. Some people rationalize: *"A small profit is better than no profit."* That is **wrong**.

The key to trading success is making sure your **average win is greater than your average loss**. Even with a 50% win rate, you can still be profitable if your average win is 2R and your average loss is 1R.

But fear-driven traders exit trades at 0.5R when the system says 2R. If your wins are 0.5R and your losses are 1R, you will lose money even with five wins and three losses in a sample of eight — because your wins cannot cover your losses. **The system no longer has positive expectancy.**

**Example:** Three consecutive losing trades (-3R). You feel you have to lock in any profit you get. On the next valid signal, the trade reaches 1.2R and starts to pull back — *"I've seen this movie before, it's going to come down and take my profit."* You sell at 0.8R. The price then goes on to hit 2R. **You should have made 2R; you actually made 0.8R.** Repeated over six trades, this small-profit behavior destroys your edge.

### 7.8.3 Hope

> "In real life, it's good to have hope. You hope your kids will study hard. You hope your wife won't leave you. But **in trading, you can never have hope. In trading, hope is for the hopeless. Hope will cause you to lose everything you have.**"

**Example:** You buy a stock with a stop loss at, say, $8.05. The price drops, hits your stop, and immediately reverses up to your profit target. You feel cheated: *"I was right on the direction — it just temporarily hit my stop."* Next time you either skip the stop loss or move it further away. Now when the next trade goes against you, you move the stop loss even further to "give yourself more room to be right." Eventually, instead of losing 1R, you lose **4R or 5R**.

This is why some traders are right **80% of the time** and still lose money: on the 8 winners they take small profits, but on the 2 losers they hold and hold, hoping to be right, and the catastrophic losses wipe out all their profits.

### 7.8.4 Doubt

Doubt and uncertainty after a series of losses cause traders to **freeze when the next valid signal appears**. Their hands shake; they cannot pull the trigger. If you cannot take the next trade, **you will not succeed**. If you keep taking the next trade, eventually you will succeed. Never be paralyzed by an inevitable losing streak.

### 7.8.5 The Need to Be Right

Predicting the trade outcome and becoming attached to being right makes it almost impossible to cut losses cleanly. Trading is not about being right — it is about **making money**. Detach yourself emotionally from each individual trade outcome and have **no expectations** when you place it.

## 7.9 Handling Winning and Losing Streaks

### 7.9.1 The Shape of Your Equity Curve

A typical equity curve over 100 trades might start at $10,000 and grow to roughly $15,000–$20,000 — but the growth **is not a straight line**. It goes up and down, just like the price of a stock.

* The overall outcome of your trading journey **will not unfold linearly**.
* Do **not** look at the trading outcomes of a small period and extrapolate them into the future.

### 7.9.2 Don't Extrapolate Streaks

* If you happen to hit a losing streak of 5–10 in a row, **don't get freaked out**. Some traders see ten losses and think *"If this keeps going, I'll lose everything."* That is extrapolation — and it is wrong.
* If you get a winning streak of 10–15 trades, **don't get too excited**. Don't think *"I'll be a millionaire next week."*

In the long run, **winning and losing streaks tend to even out**. The long-run distribution of wins and losses is what makes you profitable. **Long-run performance has little or nothing to do with luck.** Short-term results contain a lot of luck (5 wins in a row or 4 losses in a row); over time, luck evens out and the long run is all about **statistics and probability**.

## 7.10 Common Emotional Mistakes Made by New Traders

A summary of patterns to be aware of and avoid:

1. **Rushing into new trades** after a few losses, trying to win the money back quickly. This causes traders to take low-quality trades that do not meet their rules.
2. **Becoming paralyzed** by doubt and uncertainty after losing streaks, so they cannot pull the trigger on the next valid signal.
3. **Staying in a losing trade** hoping things will turn around — "there is no hope in the market, hope is for the hopeless."
4. **Focusing on short-term results** and losing perspective. Think about the big picture.
5. **Failing to stick to a consistent risk-per-trade percentage**. Don't raise risk when greedy or lower it when fearful.
6. **Constantly changing trading systems or strategies** after short-term losses. This is the hallmark of the search for the "holy grail." Traders hop from forex to options to futures to stocks and never stick with one system long enough for it to work its statistical magic.
7. **Focusing on the account balance** instead of following the system. Watching the balance makes you emotional and causes you to deviate.
8. **Getting emotionally affected by losses** — stressed, fearful, frustrated, losing confidence.
9. **Blaming external reasons** for losses: *"The market is bad, the Fed raised rates, Trump won the election."* As long as you blame others, you will never succeed — there will always be something to blame.
10. **Taking revenge on a stock or the market** after a loss — holding a losing stock or buying more of it until "it gives me my money back." **The stock doesn't know who you are. Don't get personal.**
11. **Trading with methods that don't suit your personality** — copying someone else's system out of envy. Choose a system that fits your personality.

## 7.11 Habits of Successful Traders

A summary of the habits shared by winning traders:

1. **Take action** when there is a valid, high-probability signal in their trading system. Don't let fear control their decisions.
2. **Take responsibility**, learn from mistakes, and move on. Don't dwell on setbacks — be like Dory from *Finding Dory*: just **"keep on swimming."** Wins and losses should not affect your psychology of taking the next trade.
3. **Expect and accept losses** as part of the trading game. You have to lose to win. Focus on following the trading system, not on the immediate balance.
4. **Think statistically** and keep a long-term perspective.
5. **Focus on consistency** — consistently follow the predetermined entry and exit rules and don't listen to other people.
6. **Be patient** — wait only for high-probability opportunities.

> "The biggest enemy to your trading success is **not** the market. **It's you.**"

A good trade is one where **you follow the rules of the system** — not one that necessarily led to a short-term profit.

## 7.12 Dealing with Drawdowns

### 7.12.1 What Drawdowns Are

A **drawdown** is a reduction in your account equity from a losing trade or a series of losing trades — both of which are inevitable. Drawdowns are the **cost of being in the trading business** and the **main cause of psychological damage** to traders.

As you trade, your equity will not go up in a straight line — it will go through ups and downs. Every dip (e.g., from $15,000 to $12,000, or from $22,000 to $17,000) is a drawdown.

### 7.12.2 Four Strategies for Handling Drawdowns Psychologically

**1. Condition your mind to accept drawdowns** as a normal part of the trading journey — the normal cost of doing business.

Think of trading as a business with **sales and costs**:

* **Win = sales.** A 2R win is the sale.
* **Loss = cost of doing business.** A −1R loss is the cost.

If you win half the time (2R sales) and lose half the time (−1R costs), the gross profit is still **+1R per round trip**. Look at losing trades as the **cost of business**, not as a bad thing — and you will feel great about them.

**2. Do not focus on monetary value; focus on R-multiples.**

Many beginners say *"I just lost $5,000! I just lost $500! I just lost a TV set, an iPhone!"* and quit. Instead, never think in terms of dollars — think in terms of **R-multiples**:

* A losing trade is **−1R**.
* A winning trade is **+2R** or more.

Thinking in R-multiples **shields you psychologically from the money**.

**3. Treat small samples as statistically insignificant.**

In the short term, a trade outcome is random — you cannot tell a win from a loss. In the long term, the system is always profitable. **Condition yourself to think long-term.**

**4. Master one setup.**

> "I fear not the man who has practiced 10,000 kicks once, but I fear the man who has practiced just one kick 10,000 times."
> — Bruce Lee

Successful traders tend to trade with **only a single strategy**, or at most **two**, and they do it **consistently** until they master those setups. Losing traders fire every setup they see — a breakout, a dip-buy, a divergence, a Bollinger Band overshoot — without mastering any of them.

If you keep doing many different kinds of "kicks" without mastering anything, you cannot make money consistently. Focus on **one or at most two systems**, repeat them over and over with **no deviation**, and you will be extremely profitable over time.

## 7.13 Key Takeaways

* **Psychology is more important than strategy.** Most of trading success (60%+) comes from psychological discipline — the ability to follow your system consistently without deviation.
* **Trade statistically.** Any single trade is random. Focus on following the rules and on the long-run distribution of wins and losses, not on any single outcome.
* **Ignore news, rumors, and predictions.** You cannot predict the future, and the market's interpretation of news is also unpredictable.
* **Stay disciplined.** Risk a fixed percentage per trade (1%–3%). Don't raise or lower it based on how you feel.
* **Trade good trades only.** Good = following the rules. The outcome (win or loss) is irrelevant to whether the trade was good.
* **Detach emotionally.** Think in R-multiples, not dollars. Treat losses as the cost of doing business. Don't get greedy, fearful, hopeful, or revengeful.
* **Be patient and consistent.** Wait for high-probability setups, master one or two strategies, and execute them without deviation over hundreds of trades.
* **The biggest enemy in trading is you.** All the best systems in the world will fail if you cannot follow them.
