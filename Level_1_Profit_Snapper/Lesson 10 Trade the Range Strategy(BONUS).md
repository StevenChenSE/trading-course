## 13.1 Strategy Overview and Expected Performance

The Range Trading Strategy is a **short-term swing trading strategy** designed for stocks that are trading inside a clearly defined range (also called a channel or consolidation pattern), with a strong, tested resistance level on top and a strong, tested support level on the bottom.

The strategy is directional and depends on the broader market environment:

* **Index is bullish** — S&P 500 is trading **above its 20 EMA** → **buy stocks** when they bounce off the strong **support** level of the range. Target the **resistance** at the top of the range.
* **Index is bearish** — S&P 500 is trading **below its 20 EMA** → **short stocks** when they bounce off the strong **resistance** level of the range. Target the **support** at the bottom of the range.

When executed properly, this strategy has produced these historical results for Adam:

* **Win rate:** 60–70% on average.
* **Average reward-to-risk:** 2.5R–3R (i.e. you typically risk 1R to make 2.5R–3R).
* **Frequency:** approximately **5–10 trades per month**, depending on market conditions and how selective you are.

> *"This strategy has a win rate of 60–70% on average, an average reward-to-risk of 2.5R–3R, which means we are risking 1R to make 2.5R or 3R. So it's a very good risk-win ratio. You can frequently get anywhere from 5 trades a month to some 10 trades a month depending on the month."*

Because the reward-to-risk is so favorable, the strategy is forgiving: even if you are right only **3 out of 10 times** you can still be profitable; 4 out of 10 makes you very profitable; and at the 60% win rate you are extremely profitable.

## 13.2 Range Definition — What a Tradeable Range Looks Like

The first (and most important) step is identifying a valid range. A range is **not** valid just because two horizontal lines look right; it must meet four structural requirements.

### 13.2.1 Resistance must be tested at least three times

The resistance line (top of the range) must be touched **at least three times** for it to qualify as a *strong* resistance level.

* Three or more tests = a strong resistance.
* Two tests or fewer = **not** a strong resistance; do not trade it.

> *"The resistance level needs to be tested at least three times to be considered strong resistance. So I want to see three tests right. For example, this is test one, okay two, and that's three, and that's four. So at least three tests. Less than that — that's not a strong resistance level."*

### 13.2.2 Support must also be tested at least three times

The support line (bottom of the range) must be touched **at least three times** as well.

* The rule is symmetric: **three-test resistance + three-test support** before you trade it.
* Anything less is rejected.

> *"On the support we tested three times as well. Test 1, test 2, and test 3. So that is critical: three-test resistance, three-test support."*

### 13.2.3 Price must move smoothly from one end to the other

The price action inside the range must look like a **ping-pong**: each touch of resistance is followed by a full move down to support, and each touch of support is followed by a full move up to resistance. The instructor calls this a **"smooth"** or **"very obvious"** range.

What counts as *not* smooth:

* A resistance test that fails halfway back down (does not reach the prior support level) before reversing again. This is not a convincing test.
* Price that chops sideways between intermediate levels instead of moving from one end of the range to the other.

> *"I want to see that the price has moved smoothly from one end to the other, like a ping-pong. So for example, if I see test 1, test 2 — ideally after hits two I want it to come all the way back down again and then test it again. That's why I consider it a strong test. I want to see going all the way up, test, all the way down, test. I want to see this very smooth thing. If it just does test 1 like that and goes down a bit and tests again — well, that's not very convincing."*

### 13.2.4 No intermediate support/resistance inside the range

There must be **no internal S/R levels** between the top and bottom of the channel. If the chart shows the stock going sideways mid-channel, it means there is a sub-level of resistance or support hiding inside the channel — and that sub-level may stop your trade before it reaches the target.

> *"Sometimes you could see price go sideways like that, and then up, and then come down and go sideways again. It tells you that within this resistance and support there could be another level of support and resistance. So if I'm buying here, you may not smoothly go back to the top — you may just go to the mid and come back down again. This is a one-on-one — I want to see a very smooth thing."*

### 13.2.5 Direction of the prior trend

For **long trades**, Adam prefers ranges where the stock had been in an **uptrend** before going into consolidation. The idea is that the underlying buying bias is still upward, and the consolidation is just a pause.

> *"When you're going long in this pattern, which means you're buying at the support, preferably you want the price to have been on an uptrend before the consolidation pattern. In other words it was on an uptrend before; now it's in consolidation. I prefer to go long on these stocks because the buyers are still — upward is still an upward bias that is going to probably continue."*

Bonus confirmations (not strict rules) when going long:

* **50 MA > 150 MA** tells you the overall trend is up.
* Price **above the 200 MA** is also a bonus that confirms an upward bias.

During consolidation the moving averages often criss-cross sideways; that is normal. Do not be put off by messy MA crossovers inside the channel.

### 13.2.6 Patience and selectivity

Adam reviews **50–100 charts a day** and frequently **takes no trades** because no setup is clean enough. Do not force trades.

> *"I look at potential charts every single day — every day I mean, you know, anywhere from 50 to 100 of these charts and not even choose one. I'm very discerning, so you're gonna be very patient. Only choose the one that's very clear, very obvious. Don't force the trade. Don't take a trade just because you want to hit five a month or 10 a month. There's no high-quality trade."*

## 13.3 Long Trade Entry Rules

### 13.3.1 Trigger candle

Adam enters a long trade when one of the following appears **at the support level of the range**:

* **The first bullish (green) candle** that appears near the support after a wave of red candles. The bullish candle must be near or touching the support.
* **A bullish pin bar** at the support — because pin bars are powerful reversal candles, Adam is willing to enter on the pin bar itself rather than wait for a follow-up bullish candle.

> *"What that means is that we have got that sideways pattern. So you want to see at least test 1, test 2, test 3. As it's coming down, what happens? Usually when it comes down, you see bearish candles — red candles. The moment it comes near this support level, the moment I see a first green candle — which is a bullish candle — the moment I see that bullish candle close, I'll probably take an entry."*

> *"Or sometimes if I see a bullish pin bar — small candle body with a long lower shadow — I would already jump in and take a trade even without a bullish candle, because usually a bullish pin bar is a strong reversal pattern."*

The bullish candle (or pin bar) **must close above the recent swing low** that market makers may have just swept. This is a structural filter:

> *"You must close above the recent low. When I enter I must buy above the low of this point. Because if it goes down like that and the candle appears here, this support could be a potential resistance."*

### 13.3.2 Order placement (long)

| Order | Type | Placement |
|-------|------|-----------|
| **Entry** | Buy Stop Limit Order | A few cents **above the high** of the bullish candle (or bullish pin bar) that triggered the entry. |
| **Stop loss** | Sell Stop Order | A few cents **below the lowest point of the channel** (i.e. the lowest low of the entire range). |
| **Profit target** | Sell Limit Order | At the **previous resistance** of the range (top of the channel). |

### 13.3.3 Cents-above / cents-below rule (price-based offset)

How many cents you place the entry above the trigger candle's high — or the stop below the channel low — depends on the stock's price:

| Stock price | Cents above/below the trigger |
|-------------|-------------------------------|
| Less than $5 | **1 cent** |
| $5–$10 | **2 cents** |
| $10–$50 | **3 cents** |
| $50–$100 | **4 or 5 cents** |
| Above $100 | **10 cents** |

> *"It depends on the price of the stock. If it's less than a five-dollar stock I usually use 1 cent. If it's a $5 to $10 stock I would use 2 cents. A $10 to $50 stock I would usually use 3 cents. If it's a $50 to $100 stock I'll use 4 or 5 cents. Anything above $100 I'll use 10 cents."*

### 13.3.4 Limit price (to prevent slippage)

After you calculate 1R (entry minus stop), the limit price is **0.1R above the entry price**. For a $50 stock where 1R = $0.61, 0.1R = $0.06, so the limit price is set 6 cents above the stop entry price.

> *"So 0.1R is 6 cents. So our limit price would be 6 cents above the stop price. So $50.56 plus 6 cents = $50.62."*

### 13.3.5 Stop-loss buffer (ATR rule)

When the **current low of the range is the lowest low of the range** (i.e. the support has just been printed), the buffer rule matters most:

* Make sure the stop loss is at least **1 ATR below the support**.
* If not, push the stop loss further out so the buffer is at least 1 ATR.

> *"Ensure that our stop loss is at least 1 ATR below the support. You can always calculate your ATR to ensure that your price is at least 1 ATR below the support."*

### 13.3.6 Risk-to-reward target

The strategy looks for trades where the risk-to-reward is at least **1R : 2R**, but most setups naturally give **1R : 3R** or **1R : 4R**.

> *"My target would be the high of the channel — my target price. So I want to make sure that I get at least a 1:2 reward-to-risk ratio. Most of the time it's more than 2 — usually it's like 3 or 4R. But at least 2R for it to be worth my while."*

## 13.4 Short Trade Entry Rules

Short trades are the mirror image of long trades and are taken when the S&P 500 is below its 20 EMA.

### 13.4.1 What matters most in a short

For shorts, **resistance is more important than support**:

* The resistance line must be **tested at least three times** (the more times, the stronger). Adam has counted four and even five tests in some examples.
* The support should ideally also be tested three times, but if it breaks below support the short just makes even more profit, so support quality matters less than for a long.

> *"When you're going short, what's more important — the resistance or the support? If you're going short the resistance is more important, because you want to make sure it's a strong resistance that will reverse the price to go back down again."*

### 13.4.2 Trigger candle (short)

The short trigger is **the first bearish candle or bearish pin bar that appears at the resistance level**. A **bearish pin bar** at the resistance — especially one that is **penetrating the upper Bollinger Band** — is the highest-probability trigger because it signals the price is overbought.

> *"I'm looking for the first bearish candle or bearish pin bar that appears at the resistance level. The highest-probability trade occurs when during the price hitting the resistance, it is also penetrating the upper Bollinger Bands, which tells us that the price is overbought and ready to reverse."*

### 13.4.3 Order placement (short)

| Order | Type | Placement |
|-------|------|-----------|
| **Entry** | Sell Stop Limit Order | A few cents **below the low** of the bearish candle (or bearish pin bar). |
| **Stop loss** | Buy Stop Order | A few cents **above the high of the channel** (highest point of the range). |
| **Profit target** | Buy Limit Order | At the **previous support** of the range. |

### 13.4.4 Stop-loss buffer (short)

When the **current high of the range is the highest point**, the stop loss must be **at least 1 ATR above the resistance**. If not, push it higher until the buffer is at least 1 ATR.

> *"We then ensure that the stop loss is at least 1 ATR above our resistance. If it is not, then you have to push it a bit higher to get at least 1 ATR."*

### 13.4.5 Limit price (short)

For shorts the limit price is **0.1R below the stop price** (entry).

> *"0.1R would be 6 cents. So when we're going short we place our limit order 0.1R below the stop price. So $18.29 minus 6 cents = $18.23."*

## 13.5 Stop-Hunt Re-Entry Rule (Market-Maker Flush)

Adam's **favorite high-probability long setup** within a range happens when market makers push price down through the recent low, taking out stop-losses of buyers who entered earlier, before reversing back up.

Pattern to recognize:

* Price makes a low at support.
* Goes up slightly.
* Drops again, making a **slightly lower low** that triggers stop-losses below the prior swing low.
* Then reverses back up sharply.

What to do:

1. **Do not buy immediately** on the lower-low candle.
2. **Wait for a bullish candle that closes above the recent low** (above the line that was just swept).
3. Once that bullish candle appears, enter on a buy-stop just above its high.
4. Stop loss still goes below the lowest point of the channel.

> *"That's how this pattern is created. It makes a low, goes up slightly, makes a slightly lower low, and then zoom — it goes all the way back up. So I say that this takes out the stop losses placed by the buyers over here. And I do entries when the price hits and takes out stop losses from a recent low — but you must close above the recent low."*

**Re-entry after a stop-loss:** If your stop gets hit by the flush and price immediately reverses back above the support, **get back in again**. The flush was a stop hunt, not a real breakdown.

> *"When I notice it flushes out people's stops, if I get taken out and it reverses back up, I get back in. So the first one could have been a loss — I lose 1R. But when I get back in again I could make two or three R. So in the end I still make a net profit from there."*

Worked example: a losing trade that loses 1R followed by a winning trade that makes 3R still nets 2R across the pair.

## 13.6 Up-Trending Channels (Special Case)

Most of the rules above apply to **horizontal channels**. But the same logic works on **up-trending channels** (rising support and rising resistance) with one important change.

### 13.6.1 Definition

* Higher lows, higher lows, higher highs.
* Draw a **trending support line** that passes through at least **three swing lows** before you enter.

### 13.6.2 Stop placement (key difference)

For an up-trending channel, do **not** put the stop loss below the lowest point of the entire channel — that would make 1R unacceptably wide. Instead:

* Place the stop loss **a few cents below the current low** (the most recent swing low used to draw the trendline).
* Ensure the stop is still at least **1 ATR below the support** so market makers cannot easily take you out.

> *"Whenever you've got an up-trending channel, you simply put the stop loss a few cents below the current low — not the lowest low, but the current low — unlike the horizontal channel."*

This is the only structural difference. Entry and profit target rules stay the same.

## 13.7 Worked Trade Examples (Long)

### 13.7.1 REGL — Long Setup

* **Range:** Resistance tested 3–4 times; support tested 3 times. Smooth ping-pong between the two levels.
* **Trigger:** After a series of red candles, a green candle appears at the support.
* **Entry:** Buy stop limit order **3 cents above the high of the green candle** at **$50.56** (a $50 stock). Limit price set at **$50.62** (0.1R above).
* **Stop loss:** A few cents below the lowest low of the range. Because the current low was the lowest low, pushed down further to maintain a **1 ATR buffer**. Final stop = **$49.95**.
* **1R:** $50.56 − $49.95 = **$0.61 per share**.
* **Profit target:** Previous resistance = **$52.65**.
* **Reward:** $52.65 − $50.56 = **$2.09**.
* **Risk-to-reward:** $2.09 / $0.61 = **3.4R**.
* **Outcome:** Order not triggered next day; triggered the day after; price pulled back (a market-maker flush that **just missed the stop** thanks to the 1 ATR buffer); then ran up to hit the profit target.

### 13.7.2 STLD — Long Setup

* **Range:** Three tests of resistance and three tests of support. Includes a textbook stop-hunt pattern (down, slightly up, slightly lower low, then up).
* **Trigger:** Bullish pin bar at support.
* **Entry:** Buy stop limit order **a few cents above the high of the pin bar** = **$33.34**.
* **Stop loss:** A few cents below the lowest low of the channel = **$32.20**.
* **1R:** $33.34 − $32.20 = **$1.40**.
* **Profit target:** Top of the channel = **$38.00**.
* **Reward:** $38.00 − $33.34 = **$4.66**.
* **Risk-to-reward:** **1 : 3.3R**.
* **Outcome:** Winning trade.

### 13.7.3 IRDM — Long Setup

* **Range:** Three resistance tests and three support tests, all clean.
* **Trigger:** First green candle at the support after a long run of red candles.
* **Entry:** Buy stop limit order above the high of the green candle at **$9.90**.
* **Stop loss:** A few cents below the lowest point of the range = **$9.60**.
* **1R:** $9.90 − $9.60 = **$0.30**.
* **Profit target:** Previous resistance = **$11.47**.
* **Reward:** $11.47 − $9.90 = **$1.57**.
* **Risk-to-reward:** $1.57 / $0.30 = **5.2R**.
* **Outcome:** Winning trade.

### 13.7.4 PCBK — Long Setup (Stop Hunt)

* **Range:** Three resistance tests and three support tests.
* **Trigger:** Two tests of support seen — Adam deliberately waited and **did not enter** until the third test confirmed the support.
* **Stop-hunt pattern:** A second wave down took out the stops of buyers who entered on the first wave, making a slightly lower low.
* **Entry:** A bullish green candle appeared that **closed above the recent low**; buy stop limit at **$23.51** (3 cents above the high of the green candle).
* **Stop loss:** Just below the lowest low = **$22.81**.
* **1R:** $23.51 − $22.81 = **$0.62**.
* **Profit target:** Previous resistance = **$26.58**.
* **Reward:** $26.58 − $23.51 = **$3.07**.
* **Risk-to-reward:** **1 : 4.9R**.
* **Outcome:** Winning trade. The PCBK example is highlighted as a "not a great trade" only because the trigger came right at the support flush — but it produced the highest R-multiple of any of the examples.

### 13.7.5 Up-Trending Channel Example

* **Channel:** Diagonal trending channel. Resistance tested four times; support trendline through three higher lows.
* **Trigger:** First green candle at the trending support.
* **Entry:** Buy stop limit just above the high of the green candle at **$79.90**.
* **Stop loss:** Below the current low (NOT the lowest low) = **$78.80**. Ensures 1R is reasonable.
* **1R:** $79.90 − $78.80 = **$1.10**.
* **Profit target:** Top of the channel = **$82.36**.
* **Reward:** $82.36 − $79.90 = **$2.46**.
* **Risk-to-reward:** approximately **1 : 2.5R**.
* **Outcome:** Winning trade (described as "not fantastic but still reasonable" because the 1R was kept tighter by using the current low instead of the absolute low).

## 13.8 Worked Trade Example (Short)

### 13.8.1 Short Setup — $18 Stock

* **Range:** Resistance tested at least 5 times (very strong); support tested at least 3 times. Smooth ping-pong.
* **Trigger:** Bearish pin bar at the resistance that **penetrated the upper Bollinger Band** — highest-probability short trigger.
* **Entry:** Sell stop limit order **3 cents below the low of the bearish pin bar** at **$18.29** (an $18 stock).
* **Stop loss:** A few cents above the highest point of the channel = **$18.92**. Because the current high is the channel high, pushed the stop further out to maintain a **1 ATR buffer above the resistance**.
* **1R:** $18.92 − $18.29 = **$0.63**.
* **Limit price:** 0.1R below the entry = **$18.23**.
* **Profit target:** Previous support = **$16.41** (later revised to $16.14).
* **Reward:** $18.29 − $16.41 = **$2.78** (using $16.41).
* **Risk-to-reward:** $2.78 / $0.63 = **4.4R**.
* **Outcome:** Next day the stock opened and triggered the sell stop; price went all the way down to the support target.

## 13.9 Bracket Order and Trade Management

### 13.9.1 Place all three orders at once (bracket)

Enter the trade as a **bracket order** — a buy-stop entry, a stop loss, and a profit target all submitted together. This locks in the plan the moment the setup is identified and removes the temptation to fiddle with the trade.

> *"So once I enter the trade I enter a buy-stop order with a stop loss and a profit target. So it's a bracket order. I enter all three orders at once — buy-stop and profit target."*

### 13.9.2 The +1R breakeven rule

Once price moves **+1R in your favor**, **move the stop loss to the entry price**. The trade is now risk-free; the worst-case outcome is breakeven.

> *"The moment the price moves one R in my favor, I will then shift my stop loss to the entry price. So now the worst that can happen is I break even. So once I shift my stop loss to the entry price, I leave it to hit the profit target — which could be 3R, 4R, 2R, whatever it is."*

### 13.9.3 Optional trailing stop at +2R

If you prefer to actively manage the trade:

* When price hits **+2R unrealized**, move the stop to **+1R above entry**.
* This locks in 1R of profit while still allowing the trade to run to 3R+.

> *"Once the price moves to two R — so I've got an unrealized two R profit right now — I could shift my stop loss here. So when I move my stop loss to one R above the entry price, what happens is that if it goes back down it hits my stop loss, you know, out of the trade. But at least I get 1R in my pocket."*

### 13.9.4 Defensive stop on a bearish candle near the target

If, as the trade approaches the profit target, a **bearish candle suddenly appears** while the rest of the move has been bullish, you may tighten the stop to just below the low of that bearish candle to protect unrealized gains. Let the trade continue if it keeps going up.

> *"Sometimes to protect what I have, I will shift my stop loss to just below the low of that bearish candle, a few cents below the long bearish candle. So if it continues going up, I get stopped out — I think my profits and I... if it continues going up, fine."*

## 13.10 Daily Screening Workflow (Finviz)

Adam does **not** use a custom TC2000 or Thinkorswim scan for this strategy — he uses **Finviz** because the free channel screener is sufficient.

### 13.10.1 The daily routine

1. Check the **S&P 500 / SPX** trend.
   * If SPX is **above the 20 EMA** → look for **longs** (channels where price is near or bouncing off the support).
   * If SPX is **below the 20 EMA** → look for **shorts** (channels where price is near or bouncing off the resistance).
2. Go to **Finviz → Screens** and select **"Channel"** (or **"Channel Up"** / **"Channel Down"** as appropriate).
3. Browse through the list of stocks currently in a channel. Open each chart that catches your eye.

### 13.10.2 Volume filter

Only consider stocks with **average daily volume of at least 150,000 shares** so that the position is liquid enough to enter and exit cleanly.

> *"Volume is important as well. When you're trading this you want to ensure at least 150,000 of volume. 100 shares — I mean, 150,000 shares average daily volume."*

### 13.10.3 What to avoid (red flags)

Reject the chart if any of the following are true:

| Red flag | Why it's bad |
|----------|--------------|
| **Range too narrow** | Not enough "meat" between support and resistance to make 2R+ worthwhile. |
| **Stock near the top of the range** (when looking for longs) | You want to buy at the bottom, not chase at the top. |
| **Stock has broken out of the channel** | No longer in a range; this becomes a breakout trade (covered in Level 2). |
| **Overall trend is down** (when looking for longs) | Adam prefers ranges that form after an uptrend — "an uptrend consolidation that continues the uptrend." |
| **Lots of gaps between candles** | Gappy charts do not respect support/resistance cleanly; skip them. |
| **Intermediate S/R inside the channel** | The "smooth ping-pong" rule fails. |
| **Less than three tests of one side** | Three-test rule not met. |
| **Resistance within the resistance** | If there is an obvious prior resistance level *inside* the channel between entry and target, the price is likely to stall there. |

### 13.10.4 Multiple pages

Finviz returns the channel results in multiple pages. Walk through pages 1, 2, 3, 4 — the same way Adam does — and apply the checklist above to every chart.

> *"You can look at page 3 and page 4 and do the same thing which I've done. I don't see any right now, but I hope I've given you a good idea of what I'm looking for."*

### 13.10.5 Don't force trades

It is normal — and expected — to find **no qualifying setups** on any given day. Adam himself routinely finds none and walks away. Patience is part of the edge.

> *"I couldn't find any trades today. I'm just gonna show it to you and I'll see — I'll show you what is in my mind. I don't see anymore. So I don't see any right now, but I hope I've given you a good idea of what I'm looking for when I look for trading the range trades."*

## 13.11 Filtering by Volume, Trend, and Smoothness

Beyond the three-test + smoothness rule, apply three additional filters that are easy to check on a chart:

1. **Volume:** Average daily volume ≥ 150,000. Anything thinner is too risky to enter/exit.
2. **Trend context:** Longs only when the prior trend was up; shorts only when the prior trend was down. The consolidation should be a **continuation pause**, not a counter-trend pause.
3. **Candle spacing:** Avoid gappy charts. You want the candles to flow continuously with no large empty spaces — those gaps break the support/resistance logic.

## 13.12 Trading the Opposite Direction After a Winning Long

Once a long trade has hit its target at the resistance, you can **consider** flipping to a short at the same resistance level — provided the **index context still allows it**.

* If SPX is still bullish and you are sitting at a resistance in a bullish market, going short there is **more risky**.
* If SPX is rolling over (overbought Bollinger Band penetration, or starting to come off the 20 EMA), the short becomes higher probability.

The mechanics are the same as the long in reverse: sell-stop entry just below the bearish candle's low, stop just above the channel high (with 1 ATR buffer), target the previous support.

> *"So right now the S&P is still bullish. To take a short trade now is a bit more risky. Unless the S&P starts to retrace coming down — it's overbought, coming down — yeah, you could. I'm not saying you can't, it's just a bit more risky."*

## 13.13 Common Mistakes to Avoid

| Mistake | Correct approach |
|---------|------------------|
| Buying a stock that has only been tested twice at support or resistance. | Wait for the **third test** before entering. |
| Buying a range with messy / sideways action in the middle. | Demand **smooth ping-pong** price action between the two boundaries. |
| Buying a stock that gapped up / down through the channel. | Skip gappy charts; they don't respect S/R cleanly. |
| Forcing a trade just to hit a monthly trade count. | **Don't take the trade.** Wait for the bus — there is always another bus. |
| Placing the stop loss too tight (e.g. just below the trigger candle's low). | Place the stop below the **lowest low of the channel** and ensure at least **1 ATR buffer**. |
| Chasing a stock that has already broken out of the channel. | That's a breakout trade (Level 2); not a range trade. |
| Using 0.1R increments that don't match the stock's price. | Apply the cents-above/below rule based on the stock's price tier. |
| Going long while SPX is below the 20 EMA (or short while SPX is above). | Always confirm the **index direction** before entering. |

## 13.14 Summary Checklist

### Before Entering a Range Trade

- [ ] Channel is **clearly defined** with smooth ping-pong price action.
- [ ] Resistance tested **3+ times**; support tested **3+ times**.
- [ ] **No intermediate S/R** inside the channel.
- [ ] Stock volume is **≥ 150,000** shares/day.
- [ ] SPX is **above 20 EMA for longs** / **below 20 EMA for shorts**.
- [ ] Prior trend direction matches the trade direction (uptrend → longs; downtrend → shorts).
- [ ] Candles are continuous — **no major gaps**.

### Entry Mechanics

- [ ] Wait for the first bullish/bearish candle (or pin bar) at the right edge of the range.
- [ ] If a pin bar appears, can enter on the pin bar; otherwise wait for confirmation candle.
- [ ] Buy-stop entry a few cents above the trigger (use cents-table for offset).
- [ ] Stop loss a few cents below the lowest low (horizontal range) or current low (up-trending range).
- [ ] Profit target at the opposite boundary of the range.
- [ ] Confirm **1R : 2R minimum**, ideally **1R : 3R+**.
- [ ] Set limit price at **+0.1R** above entry (longs) or **−0.1R** below entry (shorts).
- [ ] Confirm **1 ATR buffer** between stop and the relevant S/R level.

### During the Trade

- [ ] Order was placed as a **bracket** (entry + stop + target all at once).
- [ ] At **+1R**: move stop loss to entry price (breakeven).
- [ ] At **+2R** (optional): move stop to +1R to lock in profit.
- [ ] If a bearish/bullish candle appears near the target, optionally tighten stop below that candle.
- [ ] Don't chase the price; let the bracket order do its work.

### After a Stop-Out Flush

- [ ] If price reverses back through the support within the same candle or the next session, **re-enter** in the original direction.
- [ ] The first loss is usually just 1R; the re-entry can pay 2R–4R+, netting a positive cycle.
