## 8.1 Why You Need a Trading Plan

If you treat trading as a business, then just like a business you have to have a business plan — and that means **you have to have a trading plan**.

A trading plan tells you exactly **which strategies you are trading**. Because there are so many strategies you could trade, you must specify which ones you will trade and, for each one:

* The **setup** you are looking for
* The **entry rules**
* The **exit rules**
* The **position-sizing rules**
* What percentage you **risk per trade**
* How many trades you take **concurrently**
* **When you trade and when you do not trade**

Without a specific plan to give you that information, you will not get consistent results.

### Why Most Traders Fail

One of the main reasons most traders fail is that they **do not follow any plan**. They buy when they feel like buying — even when there is no clear setup. They buy because they say, *"I want to take a trip right now,"* or *"I'm feeling bored,"* or *"I want to get into the market,"* or *"I lost money on the last round, I need to go make it back really fast."*

They are driven not by a plan and not by a strategy — they are driven by **emotions**. They sell when they feel like selling, or when they are fearful. They risk more when they feel confident and less when they lose confidence. As a result, they get very inconsistent results, and their trading does not lead to a positive statistical expectancy.

> If you want to guarantee profits over a sample of trades, your system has to have a **positive statistical expectancy** — and in order to achieve that, you have to **execute the plan without deviation**.

---

## 8.2 The Trading Plan Document

Here is what the trading plan looks like. You can download it from the resource page and use it as a template to write down your specific plan.

This is how it begins — this is the *trading business plan* the instructor uses:

> "I, __________, am committed to strictly following my trading plan."

### The Daily Affirmation

It is a good idea that **every single day**, before you enter the markets and before you trade, you **read your trading plan** to frame your mind to follow it.

> "The following clearly defines my trading routine, so that every day I only look for trades based on this routine. Each trade outcome should be considered purely as a certain R-multiple, and is a sample of the larger statistical process of accumulating R-multiples over time."

In other words, you think like a business — you think like a casino, where you think in terms of **probability**, not prediction.

On a microscopic, trade-by-trade basis, each outcome is uncertain and random — every trade you enter, you have no idea whether it will be a winner or a loser. However, on a macroscopic level, over a large sample of trades based on a consistent routine that gives you a statistical edge, the aggregate outcome of many R-multiples will be **positive**, because your **average wins are more than your average loss**.

---

## 8.3 The Core Commitments of the Plan

The goal is to define a plan consisting of **unambiguous entry, exit, and risk-control criteria**. In other words, your reason for entering must be a very **objective, predefined reason** — and so must your exit reasons.

The plan gives you an **edge in the markets** because of the following:

* **Entry rules** are sufficiently stringent — they keep you away from a lot of market noise and choppy behavior, while still capturing price movements incidentally and without emphasis on prediction.
* **Exit rules** are defined to allow wins to more than cover losses in the long run, so that the **win rate is no longer the emphasis**.
* **The position-sizing method** ensures that the risk per trade is a fixed percentage of the existing account balance, allowing the accumulation of R-multiples to **compound your account** over time.

### Read This Every Single Day

This is really important — read it every single day:

> The edge provided by any trading plan must be preserved through **psychologically conditioning ourselves** to the necessary ups and downs that will happen in your account in the trading process. **New concerns about short-term fluctuations tend to diminish or eliminate the edge** that the plan is supposed to give.

Subtle shifts in trading behavior — like picking profits too early, shifting your stop losses, or entering when the rules are not clearly valid — lead to **deviations from the plan**, and that leads to a **negative expectancy**.

Once you read the plan every single day, you are clear on following it.

## 8.4 What Goes Inside the Trading Plan

Once you know that you read it every single day and are clear on following the plan, list down which strategies you will trade:

* If you are listening at **Level 1** of this course, you are learning **one strategy**, which is the **bounce strategy**.
* If you are proceeding to **Level 2**, you will be learning many more strategies, such as the **capitulation strategy**, the **Bollinger mean reversion**, the **impulse pullback**, and **day-trading / scalping strategies**.

You are going to have a lot of strategies available, but to be successful you do not have to trade them all. In fact, all you need to do is to **focus on just two strategies** (or even just one — two is plenty) that you like and that fit your personality.

During days when trades come up, **if it doesn't fit your strategy, do not take the trade** — because it does not fit your business plan.

## 8.5 The Components You Must Define

The trading plan document is broken into clear sections you must fill out:

### 8.5.1 How Many Hours per Day

How many hours a day will you spend to find trade setups? Be specific.

For example: *"Every single day from 9:00 AM to 10:00 AM I am going to look for setups and place my orders"* — or *"from 2:00 PM to 4:00 PM."*

The instructor's suggestion:

* When you first get started, allocate **at least 2 hours**.
* Once you are familiar with reading chart patterns, **30 to 45 minutes** is all you need.

### 8.5.2 Entry Rules

List down the entry rules. You have learned the entry rules for the **bounce strategy** — write them down. When you write them down, you tend to take them more seriously.

### 8.5.3 Exit Rules

Again, there are a few choices for your exit rule:

1. **Forget system.** Enter the trade with a stop-loss and target price, and let it hit either the stop-loss or the target — either a 2.5R win or a −1R loss.
2. **Shift the stop-loss to entry price** when the price moves 1R in your favour.
3. **Optional approach.** Once you are in the trade, constantly raise your stop-loss the moment you see an opposing candle (for longs, raise it when you see a bearish candle — shift the stop a few cents below the low of the bearish candle).

This second approach takes a bit more work because you keep shifting stop-losses, but the results are pretty much the same. The key is to **write down in your plan which option you will follow, and follow it consistently**.

### 8.5.4 Position-Sizing Rules

You must consistently risk the **same percentage of your capital** on every trade. A suggested path:

* **Start with 1% per trade.**
* Once you can get **consistent results** and you are confident, raise it to **2%**, and eventually **3%**.

Write it down and stick to it consistently.

### 8.5.5 Maximum Open Positions

How many open positions are you allowed to have at any one time? This depends on your own personal risk tolerance.

The instructor's personal suggestion: **never risk more than 10% of your account at any one time**.

| Risk per trade | Maximum concurrent open positions |
|----------------|-----------------------------------|
| 1%             | 10                                |
| 2%             | 5                                 |
| 3%             | 3                                 |

If you like 5% total risk, that is fine — but **write it down**. It is your business plan; it is your plan; **follow your plan**.

# Chapter 9: Trading Routine and Checklist

## 9.1 Why a Daily Routine Matters

All successful traders follow a **very consistent routine** daily in their trading process. The instructor divides his trading into two streams: **swing trading**, and **day trading or scalping**.

### Example: A Swing-Trading Routine (Trading the US Market from Singapore)

| Time (Singapore)            | Action                                                       |
|-----------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|
| 10:00 AM – 11:00 AM         | Run through the swing-trading routine (about 1 hour).        |
| 9:30 PM (US market open)    | Orders placed earlier trigger automatically.                 |

What the routine looks like in practice:

1. Look at **how the market closed the day before**. Where is the S&P 500? Is it bullish or bearish?
2. Use **TC2000** (or thinkorswim) to **screen for trade setups** based on the strategies — e.g. the bounce strategy. Screen for long setups or short setups.
3. Look at the individual charts and **pick up to two favourite setups** to trade that day.
4. **If you don't find setups to your quality liking, do not trade at all.** Only take quality setups — **a maximum of two setups per day**.
5. Once you have the two setups, place orders with your broker — **entry price, stop-loss price, target price** — *before the market opens*. When the market opens, the orders trigger automatically if they meet your conditions. **That's it.**

### Example: A Day-Trading / Scalping Routine

The day-trading routine begins at **9:00 PM Singapore time** (9:00 AM US time) — half an hour before the market opens:

1. Look for trades based on **news** and **high relative volume**.
2. Pick a **maximum of four stocks**.
3. Place orders at about **9:15 PM Singapore time**.
4. The moment the market opens, the orders trigger.
5. Exit the trades within **about two to three minutes** because it is an intraday scalp.

> "I know it very well. I'm very familiar with it, and I keep running the same routine every single day."

## 9.3 The Pre-Trade Checklist (Quick-Reference Guide)

Before running through the swing-trading routine, here is a **quick-reference guide** to ensure each trade you take meets all the key entry and exit rules.

> **Print this out. Paste it on your wall or in front of your computer.** Every time you are trading, run through the checklist consciously **until it becomes a subconscious habit**.

### 9.3.1 Step 1 — Determine the Market Bias

The first thing in the daily routine is to look at **how the market is right now**. For the US market, look at the **S&P 500**:

* **Bullish bias →** scan and filter for **long trades**.
* **Bearish bias →** scan and filter for **short trades**.
* **Choppy / sideways →** **avoid trading** — do something else (e.g. trade forex). Do not trade in a choppy market.

**Market is bullish when:**

* The S&P 500 is above the **20 EMA**, and the **20 EMA is above the 50 MA**.
* **Alternatively:** the S&P is bouncing off a strong moving-average support, with the **50 above the 150**, and the **150 above the 200**.

**Market is bearish when:**

* The S&P is hitting a strong moving-average resistance, with the **50 MA below the 150 MA**.

### 9.3.2 Step 2 — Scan for Setups

Once you have a market bias, use the **TC2000 screener** (or **thinkorswim** scanner) to scan for trade setups. For the bounce strategy learned in Level 1, scan for:

* **18 EMA bounce long**
* **50 EMA bounce long**
* **100 EMA bounce long**

(You can also scan for **150 EMA bounce long**, but the focus is usually on those three.)

For **short setups** in a bearish market: scan for **18 EMA bounce short**, **50 bounce short**, and **100 bounce short**.

Some of these criteria are **already built into the scanner**, but it is still good to confirm each one manually.

---

## 9.4 Bounce Long — Full Entry Checklist

### 9.4.1 Reversal Pattern

Take a bounce long when you have:

* A **two-candle** reversal pattern, **or**
* A **single-candle** reversal pattern, **with a confirmation candle**.

### 9.4.2 MACD

At the point of the reversal candle, ensure that **MACD (5, 100, 9)** is **bullish**. If it is bearish, it should **not have crossed bearish recently within 5 candles**.

### 9.4.3 Stochastic

At the reversal candle, ensure that **both lines of the Stochastic** are **below 30** — or recently below 30 and now **crossing back above 30** (that is fine too).

### 9.4.4 Special Rule for the 18 EMA Bounce

If you are taking an **18 EMA bounce long**, you must check **two MACDs**:

* **MACD (5, 100, 9)** must be bullish.
* **MACD (1, 8, 5, 9)** must also be bullish.

The 18 EMA bounce requires stricter conditions.

### 9.4.5 Momentum and Volume Filters

Only take **long trades on high-momentum stocks**:

* The sector is in an **uptrend**.
* The **Relative Strength indicator** is **above the red line**.
* **IBD RS ranking is above 80** (use the IBD mobile app).
* Trade stocks with **average volume of at least 150,000 shares per day**.

---

## 9.5 The Two-Out-of-Three Confluence Rule

Once a setup meets all the criteria above, it is a *valid* trade — but that **does not mean you must trade it**. Only take trades that give the **highest probability** because they give you the highest win rate and highest expected profit.

A high-probability bounce trade requires **two out of three confluence points** at the support level the price retraces to:

| Confluence # | Condition                                                                  |
|--------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| 1            | The **EMA that is hit has been recently tested before** as a strong support. |
| 2            | The price is bouncing off a **Fibonacci level** — either the **50% retracement** or the **61.8% retracement**. |
| 3            | The price is bouncing off a **horizontal support / resistance level**.     |

If two of these three occur at the same time, that gives a high-probability bounce trade.

---

## 9.6 Bounce Short — Full Entry Checklist

For short trades the logic is mirrored.

### 9.6.1 Reversal Pattern

* The price is bouncing off the moving average with a **two-candle or single-candle reversal pattern**.

### 9.6.2 MACD

* **MACD is bearish**.
* If it is not bearish, it should **not have crossed bullish within the last 5 candles**.

### 9.6.3 Stochastic

* At the bounce point, ensure that **both Stochastic lines are above 70** — or recently above 70 and now **coming back down from 70**.

### 9.6.4 Special Rule for the 18 EMA Bounce Short

* **MACD (5, 100, 9)** and **MACD (1, 8, 5, 9)** must **both be bearish**.

### 9.6.5 Momentum Filter

Go short on **low-momentum stocks**:

* The sector is on a **down trend**.
* The stock shows **low relative strength** — below the red line and falling, or with the relative strength indicator below **50** on the IBD app.

### 9.6.6 Volume Filter

Same as going long — trade **high-volume stocks** (≥ 150,000 shares/day). Low-volume stocks have wide bid-ask spreads and lots of gaps, both of which are unfavourable.

### 9.6.7 Confluence

Same two-out-of-three rule for high-probability short trades: EMA previously tested as strong resistance, Fibonacci retracement, or horizontal support/resistance.

---

## 9.7 Final Pre-Trade Checks

Before you enter a trade, run through these final filters:

### 9.7.1 Earnings Blackout

* Ensure the company is **not announcing earnings within the next 7 days**.

### 9.7.2 Avoid Biotech and Pharmaceutical Stocks

These sectors tend to **gap**:

* A failed drug trial can slam the stock down violently.
* A breakthrough finding can launch it up tremendously.

Trading gaps are dangerous — if the stock gaps against you below your stop-loss, you could end up losing **more than 1R**, because the stop-loss will not guarantee that you exit at the intended price.

> **Never trade biotech or pharma stocks with this system.** *(If you are trading with options, your risk is limited to the premium you paid, so options are fine.)*

## 9.9 Exit Rules

It is good discipline to have exit rules. Several approaches:

1. **Fixed profit target** — place a target of **1.5R to 2R above your entry**, exit when it hits, and look for the next trade.
2. **Move stop to breakeven** — once the price moves **1R in your favour**, shift the stop-loss to your entry price. The trade is now risk-free.
3. **Always exit before earnings** — the instructor exits every trade before earnings are announced, in case the stock gaps against him. *(Again, with options you can hold through earnings because risk is limited.)*
4. **Trailing stop** — instead of a fixed target, let the trade run and **shift your stop-loss up (or down for shorts) to protect profits** as the price moves in your favour.

Finally, always maintain a **consistent risk per trade** — 1%, 2%, or 3%.

# Chapter 10: Recording a Trading Journal

## 10.1 Why a Trading Journal Is Essential

One of the essential things you must have is a **trading journal** — it is included as part of the package.

> *"Why? Because when you have a trading journal, you can track every single one of your trades. And that's really important."*

Every time you have a trade, you must key it into your trading journal.

---

## 10.2 What to Record

For every trade, record the following:

| Field                  | Description                                                                                  |
|------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Long or Short**      | Is it a long or short trade?                                                                 |
| **Entry Date**         | When did you enter?                                                                          |
| **Signal / Strategy**  | What strategy got you into the trade? (e.g. BMR bounce, capitulation, impulse pullback, etc.)|
| **Entry Price**        | The price you entered at.                                                                    |
| **Stop-Loss Price**    | Where your stop-loss sits — used to calculate the **1R distance**.                          |
| **Target Price**       | Set to **2R or 2.5R**. In some strategies we target 2.5R profit; in others 2R.              |
| **Exit Price**         | Where you actually exited — either the target (a 2R win), the stop-loss (−1R loss), or a stopped-out entry price if you shifted stops to breakeven. |
| **R-Multiple Result**  | The outcome of each trade.                                                                   |
| **Monthly Total**      | Sum up the R-multiples for the month.                                                        |
| **Comments**           | Why did you take / lose the trade? Was it a *good trade* (rules followed) or *bad trade* (rules broken)? |

For example:

* 10R for the month.
* If you risk 1%, that is a **10% return**.
* If you risk 2%, that is a **20% return**.

Fill this in for every single trade. Over the year, you can see your total result.

---

## 10.3 Good Trades vs. Bad Trades

> *"In trading you may not always get a positive month. In certain months you may see −2R for the month, or −3.9R for the month. But the next month you might rebound with about +9.59R, and another +9.87R. What's important is that you keep executing valid trades."*

* A **good trade** is a trade where you **follow the rules**. A good trade could end up being either a winning trade or a losing trade. As long as you keep doing good trades, you will always make money over the long run.
* A **bad trade** is one where you **do not follow the rules** — it was not a valid system signal. You may make money in the short term on bad trades, but **in the long run you will always lose money**.

This is why it is also important to have a **comments column**:

* If you had a loss and you followed the rules → *"Good trade — cost of doing business."* No worries.
* If you had a loss and you did not follow the rules → *"Bad trade."* Note down **why** you broke the rules.

Your goal is, **as soon as possible, to have all good trades** — wins or losses, sales or costs — but ending with a **net positive profit**.

---

## 10.4 Why R-Multiples Vary in the Real World

You might ask, *"Isn't the outcome always +2R or −1R or breakeven? Why do I sometimes get −1.5R?"*

Here is the reason:

* Sometimes your stop-loss gets **gapped down below** the price you intended. If you are long and the stock gaps below the stop-loss price, you do not sell exactly at the intended stop price — you may sell **lower**, resulting in more than a 1R loss.
* Conversely, sometimes a stock **gaps up above your target price** — instead of getting 2R, you may get **2.5R**.

> *"My point is that in the short term, there's a bit of luck involved. Sometimes you're unlucky and lose more than 1R; sometimes you're lucky and get more than 2R. But in the long run, it has nothing to do with luck — because the good luck and the bad luck will even out."*

You may also see a small R like **−0.58R**: this happens when you shift your stop-loss to entry price expecting to exit at breakeven (0R), but the price gaps slightly below entry before your stop triggers.

Reality is not always as clean as in theory, but you will end up **somewhere close** — either a 2R win or a −1R loss. **As long as your average winners are larger than your average losers, you will make money in the end.**

> *"Please diligently key in your trading journal for every single trade, analyse your results, and get better with time."*

# Chapter 11: Choosing a Stock Broker

## 11.1 Why Your Broker Matters

When you want to trade in any market, you need a stock broker to access the market for you. You buy through a broker, and you sell through a broker — **your broker is your most important partner in your trading business**.

At the same time, the brokerage commissions you pay when you buy or sell shares **are the biggest cost to your trading business**. Think of your broker as being like your landlord: when you run a business, you have to pay rent to your landlord — your office rent. Commissions represent the rent you pay every month.

There are literally thousands of online brokers out there. You can search online and read about their terms and conditions, commission rates, and so on.

> **There is no perfect broker for everyone.** The right broker depends on your situation — how much capital you have, what markets you trade, what features you need.

---

## 11.2 Key Considerations When Choosing a Broker

### 11.2.1 Commission Rates and Additional Charges

Before you sign up with a broker, find out **exactly** what they charge in commissions and additional fees.

Some brokers charge a **fixed rate** per transaction (e.g. **$8 to $10 per transaction**, regardless of how many shares you buy). Examples given:

* **thinkorswim by TD Ameritrade** — fixed commission (~$8 per transaction, regardless of share count).

Other brokers charge **per share**:

* **Interactive Brokers (IB)** and **Hell Effects (HF)** — these charge **half a cent to a few cents per share**, with a **minimum of $1 to $2 per transaction**.

#### The 5% Rule for Commissions

In general, **total commissions should not exceed 5% of your capital**. Ideally, much less. If commissions consume more than 5% of your capital, you have to cover more than 5% before you are actually making money.

Commissions as a percentage of capital fall as your account grows:

* A **$10 commission** on a **$50,000 account** is small (≈ 0.02% per trade).
* A **$10 commission** on a **$2,000 account** is large (≈ 0.5% per trade).

#### Worked Example

* 20 trades per month → ~240 trades per year.
* Each round trip = buy + sell = 2 transactions per trade.
* 240 × 2 = **480 transactions per year**.
* At a **$1 minimum** per transaction = **$480 in commissions per year**.
* $480 ÷ $20,000 capital = **2.4%** of capital annually — comfortable (well under 5%).

### 11.2.2 Watch Out for Hidden Charges

Some brokers charge low commissions up front but levy **inactivity fees** if you do not trade a minimum number of times per month. Some charge for **data feeds**. Read the website thoroughly to understand all charges and commission rates.

### 11.2.3 Financial Strength of the Broker

There are many brokers that let you deposit just $200 with very low commissions. If something seems too good to be true, **it might be**:

* How many years has the broker been around?
* Is the broker **regulated**?
* Is the broker **financially stable**?

Check the financial rating of the broker on its website.

### 11.2.4 Minimum Deposit

How much do you have to deposit to begin? It varies widely:

| Broker              | Minimum deposit      |
|---------------------|----------------------|
| Interactive Brokers | $10,000 (historically; now no minimum) |
| Hell Effects        | ~$2,000              |

### 11.2.5 Margin Rates

As a trader (not a long-term investor), you will often need to use **margin**. Margin means the broker lends you extra capital so your position size can exceed your cash deposit.

* With **2× leverage** and $2,000 of capital, you can buy up to **$4,000** of shares.
* With **3× leverage** and $2,000 of capital, you can buy up to **$6,000** of shares.
* As a trader you need margin because you often use tight stop-losses and tight profit targets, which can make position sizes larger than your cash.

You will need to open either a **cash account** or a **margin account**. **The instructor's strong recommendation is to open a margin account** — because margin gives you the flexibility to take more positions, and **margin is required if you want to short**. With a cash account, you can only go long.

#### Margin Interest

Brokers do not lend you money for free. The borrowed amount is charged interest, normally quoted per annum but charged daily. Margin interest rates vary widely:

* Some brokers: **as low as 1.5%** per year.
* Others: up to **9% per year**.

Always check the broker's margin interest rate.

### 11.2.6 Trading Platform

Ensure the broker provides a trading platform with all the features you need:

* **Stop-loss orders**
* **Stop-entry orders**
* **Stop-limit orders**
* **Trailing stop orders**
* **Limit orders**
* Full range of order types

Most US and Australian brokers offer all of these.

### 11.2.7 Range of Products and Markets

The broker must give you access to the markets and products you intend to trade. The instructor trades only the US market, primarily:

* **Stocks**
* **ETFs**
* **CFDs** *(Contract for Differences — these allow you to buy shares without buying the underlying, often with 10× leverage, e.g. $10,000 of Apple with a $1,000 deposit)*

**CFDs are only available to non-US citizens** (Australians, Singaporeans, Indians, etc.). US citizens cannot use CFDs and must use options for additional leverage.

If you trade forex, futures, or bonds, ensure the broker offers those products.

### 11.2.8 Customer Service

Choose a broker with **good customer service**:

* Hotline you can call.
* Online live-chat support.
* Online video tutorials on how to use the platform.
